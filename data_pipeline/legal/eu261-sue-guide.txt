THE COMPLETE STEP-BY-STEP GUIDE TO SUING UNDER EU261

STEP 1: CONFIRM YOUR ELIGIBILITY
Before taking legal action, you must ensure your flight meets EU261 criteria.
- Route: The flight departed from an EU airport OR arrived at an EU airport on an EU-based airline.
- Delay/Cancellation: You arrived at your final destination 3+ hours late, the flight was canceled within 14 days of departure, or you were denied boarding.
- Fault: The disruption was the airline's fault (e.g., crew shortage, technical issue). It cannot be an "extraordinary circumstance" (e.g., weather, ATC strikes).

STEP 2: GATHER YOUR EVIDENCE
Courts require proof. Compile the following into a single folder:
- Booking confirmation and e-ticket.
- Boarding passes (crucial for proving you were actually at the airport).
- Written communications from the airline (texts, emails, or paper notices).
- Receipts for expenses incurred during the delay (food, hotel, transport).

STEP 3: SEND A FORMAL CLAIM TO THE AIRLINE (MANDATORY PREREQUISITE)
You cannot sue without first giving the airline a chance to pay voluntarily.
- Find the airline's official EU261 compensation form on their website.
- Submit your details and demand the specific amount you are owed (€250 for flights under 1,500km; €400 for 1,500km-3,500km; €600 for over 3,500km).
- Keep a screenshot or record of the submitted form.
- Wait. Legally, you must give the airline a reasonable time to respond (usually 6 to 8 weeks). 

STEP 4: SEND A "LETTER BEFORE ACTION"
If the airline rejects your claim (claiming extraordinary circumstances) or ignores you for 8 weeks, you must send a formal warning.
- Draft a "Letter Before Action" or "Letter Before Claim".
- State your flight details, the disruption, the law (Regulation EC No 261/2004), and the compensation owed.
- Give them a strict, final deadline to pay (usually 14 to 21 days).
- State clearly: "If payment is not received by [Date], I will initiate court proceedings without further notice."
- Send this via registered or tracked mail to their legal address.

STEP 5: DETERMINE YOUR JURISDICTION
If the deadline passes, you can file a lawsuit. Under EU law (Brussels I Regulation), you have the right to sue the airline in one of three places:
1. The country where the flight departed.
2. The country where the flight arrived.
3. The country where the airline has its registered headquarters.
Choose the jurisdiction that is most convenient for you or has the cheapest/easiest small claims process.

STEP 6: FILE THE LAWSUIT
Depending on your jurisdiction, choose the appropriate court process:

Option A: The European Small Claims Procedure (Cross-Border)
- Use this if you live in an EU country, but the airline is based in a DIFFERENT EU country.
- Download and fill out "Form A" (available on the European e-Justice Portal).
- Attach your evidence (tickets, letter before action, airline responses).
- Submit the form to your local competent court.
- The court handles the rest via mail/paperwork. You rarely have to appear in person.

Option B: National Small Claims Court (Domestic)
- Use this if you are suing in the country you reside in, or if you are using the UK court system.
- Go to the government portal (e.g., Money Claim Online in the UK).
- Fill out the digital summons, write a brief summary of the claim, and pay the court filing fee (usually between €25 and €100, which you can claim back from the airline if you win).

STEP 7: THE COURT PROCESS AND JUDGMENT
- Once served, the airline has a set number of days (usually 14 to 30) to respond to the court.
- Often, just receiving the official court summons is enough to make the airline's legal department settle the case and pay you immediately to avoid court costs.
- If they defend it, a judge will review the evidence (usually on paper, but occasionally via a short hearing). 
- If you win, the court will issue a legally binding judgment ordering the airline to pay your compensation plus your court filing fees.

STEP 8: ENFORCEMENT (IF NECESSARY)
- If the airline still refuses to pay after a judge orders them to, you can hire court bailiffs to enforce the judgment (e.g., seizing assets from the airline's local office or freezing their local bank accounts).
